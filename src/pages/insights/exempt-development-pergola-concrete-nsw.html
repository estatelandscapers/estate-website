Pergolas, decks, paths and driveways: what is exempt development in NSW?

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 7 November 2026

The outdoor structure and the hard surfaces are usually the two things a new-build owner adds first. Both can be exempt development, and both have limits that are easy to pass without noticing, usually on size.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of the exempt development rules in the NSW State Environmental Planning Policy (Exempt and Complying Development Codes) 2008 (the Codes SEPP) as we understand them at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, the rules change, and your lot can carry conditions (heritage, bushfire, flood, easements, covenants) that take it outside the exempt rules. Read the current code on the NSW legislation website and the NSW Planning development types guide, or check with your council or a certifier. Pergolas, decks, patios and verandahs

The General Exempt Development Code (balconies, decks, patios, pergolas, terraces and verandahs) allows one of these to be built without approval if it:

  • has an area of not more than 25 m²
  • together with all other such structures on the lot, totals no more than 25 m² on a lot of 300 m² or less, or 15% of the dwelling’s ground floor area on a larger lot
  • is not more than 3 m high at its highest point above existing ground, with a floor no more than 1 m above existing ground and any enclosing wall no higher than 1.4 m
  • is behind the building line of any road frontage
  • is at least 900 mm from each side and rear boundary (5 m in rural and R5 zones)
  • if roofed and attached to the house, does not go above the house’s roof gutter line, and drains its roof water into the existing stormwater system (recent changes also limit the roof overhang, 600 mm)
  • is non-combustible if it is on bushfire prone land within 5 m of the dwelling
  • uses low-reflective, factory pre-coloured metal

A pergola with a roof, a deck more than a metre off the ground, or anything over 25 m² goes to a complying development certificate or a DA. The Housing Code lets a certifier approve larger attached decks and verandahs on most residential lots.

Concrete paths, paving and driveways

Two subdivisions cover hard surfaces: pathways and paving, and driveways and hard stand spaces. Together, in a residential or rural zone, the exempt limits are:

  • the total of all driveways, hard stand, paths and paving on the lot is no more than 15% of the lot area or 150 m², whichever is less
  • surface water is drained to the existing stormwater system, not onto the neighbour or the street
  • any cut or fill is no more than 600 mm above or below existing ground
  • the landscaped area in front of the building line is kept: 25% of that area where the lot is 18 m wide or less, 50% where it is wider
  • a hard stand space is at least 2.6 m by 5.4 m, no more than 20 m², 1 m behind the building line and 900 mm from side and rear boundaries
  • a driveway is no wider than the garage, carport or hard stand it serves, and is built to AS/NZS 2890.1

One thing the exempt rules do not cover is the crossover. The part of the driveway between your boundary and the kerb is on council land, and needs the roads authority’s written consent under section 138 of the Roads Act regardless of what is happening on your lot. Where people get caught

  • A double driveway plus an alfresco slab plus side paths is often more than 150 m² on a Hills block, which takes the whole lot of paving out of exempt.
  • A roofed pergola attached to the house that sits above the gutter line.
  • Paving the front yard past the landscaped-area percentage, which also affects the Occupation Certificate on a new build.
  • Slabs poured without a fall or a drain, sending water to the neighbour: a drainage failure makes the work non-exempt and is the first thing a neighbour complains about.

Questions people ask

  • Does a concrete slab for a pergola count towards the 25 m²?

    The 25 m² is the structure. The slab under it counts towards the 15% or 150 m² paving limit. Both limits apply at once.

  • Can I build a freestanding pergola in the front yard?

    Not as exempt development: the structure must be behind the building line of any road frontage. A front structure needs a CDC or a DA.

  • What about exposed aggregate or coloured concrete?

    The finish does not change the rules. Area, drainage and the landscaped-area percentage decide exempt status, not the surface.

  • Is a timber deck treated the same as a concrete patio?

    Yes, the subdivision covers balconies, decks, patios, pergolas, terraces and verandahs together, with the same area, height and setback limits.
