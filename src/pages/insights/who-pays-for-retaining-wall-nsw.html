Who pays for a retaining wall in NSW?

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 7 November 2026

Retaining walls start more neighbour arguments than fences do, because the fence rules are written down and the wall rules mostly are not. The short version: the wall belongs to whoever changed the ground, the Dividing Fences Act usually does not help, and the cost is settled by agreement or, failing that, by a court rather than the tribunal.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how responsibility for retaining walls is generally treated in NSW, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, the law and the case law change, and your title can carry easements, restrictions or covenants that change the answer. A retaining wall is not a fence

The Dividing Fences Act 1991 is the law that makes neighbours share the cost of a dividing fence. Its definition of a fence expressly leaves out a retaining wall. The one exception is a wall that is necessary to support and maintain a dividing fence: then the wall work can be treated as fencing work, shared equally to a sufficient standard, and the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NCAT) can hear it, but only with a structural engineer’s evidence that the wall is holding the fence up. Legal Aid NSW puts it plainly: NCAT will not hear a retaining wall matter without that evidence, and most retaining wall disputes end up in the Supreme Court.

The general rule: whoever changed the ground

Land in its natural state is entitled to support from the land beside it. The Conveyancing Act 1919 (section 177) turns that into a duty of care: an owner must not do anything on their land that removes or reduces the support it gives to the neighbour’s land. So:

  • If you cut your block down, you have taken away your neighbour’s support, and the wall that puts it back is yours to build and keep up.
  • If you fill your block up, the wall that keeps your fill off your neighbour is yours too.
  • If the slope is natural and nobody has changed it, nobody is obliged to build anything. Either owner may, on their own land, at their own cost.
  • A wall that is built badly and fails to provide the support can itself be a breach of the duty, which is what happened in a 2022 Supreme Court case where a new boundary wall had to be rebuilt and damages were left open.

The rule follows the earthworks, not the fence line. On a sloping block the same boundary can have a cut at one end and a fill at the other, with two different answers.

New estates and new builds

On a subdivision the developer cuts and fills every lot to create flat pads, and builds the boundary walls as part of the civil works. Those walls are then sold with the lots. Which lot owns and maintains each wall is a title question: the deposited plan, the section 88B instrument (easements for support, positive covenants) and the contract of sale. In many Sydney estates the wall is simply on the filled lot’s side of the boundary and belongs to that lot.

On a new build the builder’s cut and fill creates the need for retaining, but retaining is usually excluded from the building contract, and the Occupation Certificate can hinge on it. That is the gap our budget article and OC article cover. The neighbour who has not built yet owes nothing towards it.

Walls on the boundary

Build the wall on your own land. A wall across the line is an encroachment, and the cheapest fix is a survey peg before the excavator arrives. Where both owners want one wall on the line, agree in writing who owns it, who maintains it, how the cost is split and what happens when it fails, before it is built. We quote retaining per metre at three tiers so that conversation is about a number.

Keep drainage on your side of the wall. A wall that redirects water onto the neighbour creates a second dispute on top of the first.

If it turns into a dispute

  • Talk, then write. Set out what the wall does, who changed the levels, and what you are proposing.
  • Get an independent structural engineer’s report: whether the wall is needed, what it supports, whether it supports a fence, and what it will cost to fix. Every later step depends on it.
  • Try mediation through a Community Justice Centre; it is free and most wall disputes settle there.
  • If the wall supports a dividing fence, a fencing notice and then NCAT. If it does not, the forum is the Supreme Court, which is slow and expensive, so get legal advice before you start.

Questions people ask

  • My neighbour excavated for a new house and my yard is slumping. Who fixes it?

    The owner who removed the support generally has to restore it and make good the damage. Photograph everything, get an engineer’s report, write to the neighbour, and get legal advice before anyone builds. Do not build the wall yourself and hope to recover the cost later.

  • The developer built the wall on my boundary. Is it mine?

    Usually it sits on, and is the responsibility of, the lot it was built on, which is often the filled lot. Check the deposited plan, the section 88B instrument and your contract. Some estates record easements for support or maintenance obligations; some say nothing, and the wall goes with the land it stands on.

  • Can I make my neighbour pay half?

    Only where the wall is necessary to support and maintain a dividing fence, and then only for a sufficient standard. Otherwise a contribution is by agreement. A written agreement before the wall is built is worth far more than an argument after.

  • Does the fence on top change anything?

    It brings the fence part under the Dividing Fences Act (shared, sufficient standard) and, if the wall is genuinely needed to hold the fence up, the wall as well. An engineer has to say so in writing for NCAT to look at it.

  • Do I need approval?

    Often not for a wall up to 600 mm that meets the exempt standards; above that it is engineered and goes through a certifier or council. Our exempt development article sets out the limits.
