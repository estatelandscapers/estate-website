How high can the garden and paving sit against the house?

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 7 November 2026

The most common fault we fix on a landscaped new build is not a wall or a drain. It is dirt and mulch piled against the house, over the weepholes and the damp-proof course, because nobody set a level before the garden went in. The house tells you where the lines are. This is how to read them, and the sketch we set out from.

A summary, not advice

This article is generic information about finished levels around a house in NSW, drawn from the National Construction Code Housing Provisions and the Australian Standards it calls up, as we understand them at the date of publishing. It may or may not apply to your property: the code changes, soil class, rainfall area, termite management, flood level and the builder’s own details can all move the numbers, and the figures on your building plans and in your builder’s handover documents take precedence over anything here. Read the current NCC Housing Provisions on drainage (Part 3.3) and weatherproofing masonry (Part 5.7), and the NSW Building Commission note on damp-proof courses. Four lines on the wall

Stand at the outside of the house and there are four levels you are working to, from the top down:

  • The slab top (the inside floor level). Under the NCC Housing Provisions the slab must sit at least 150 mm above finished ground, which can drop to 100 mm in sandy, well-drained or low-rainfall ground, and to 50 mm above paving or concrete that falls away from the house.
  • The damp-proof course (DPC), the membrane in the brickwork a few courses up from the slab, with the flashing. It has to stay above the outside level: generally 150 mm above finished ground, reducing to 75 mm above paved, concreted or landscaped areas that slope away from the wall, and lower only where the wall is sheltered by a carport or verandah. Bury the DPC and the brick below it wicks water into the wall.
  • The weepholes: the open perpend joints in the course above the flashing, at least 50 mm high and no more than 1.2 m apart. They drain the cavity. They must stay open and above the outside level, and not be filled by mulch, soil, render or a paver laid against them.
  • The slab edge: where the slab edge is the termite barrier (most NSW new builds), AS 3660.1 wants 75 mm of exposed slab edge kept visible so an inspection can see any termite tracks. Garden soil over the edge defeats the barrier.

Paving against the house

Paving and concrete can come closest, because they shed water. Lay the first course 50 mm below the slab top, with a fall of 50 mm over the first metre away from the wall (25 mm is allowed for impermeable surfaces in low-rainfall areas, which most of Sydney is not). That keeps the DPC 75 mm clear on a standard brick setout and keeps the weepholes open. Where the paving has to meet a door threshold, the threshold is the slab top, so the paving still steps down from it; a grated channel drain at the door is how a level threshold is done, not raising the paving.

Garden beds and lawn against the house

Soil and mulch hold water against the wall, so they stay lower: the top of the mulch, not the soil under it, at least 150 mm below the slab top and below the weepholes and the DPC. That is why we do not run a garden bed hard against a brick wall. The detail that works is a 300 to 400 mm gravel or paved strip along the wall, the bed starting past it, and an ag drain in the gravel where the yard falls towards the house. The strip is also where the termite inspection happens.

The fall, and where the water goes

Levels only work with a fall. Every finished surface in the first metre from the house falls away from it, and from there the yard keeps falling to a pit, a swale or the street, never back to the slab. On a block that slopes towards the house the first metre still falls away; a drain or a retaining wall picks the water up beyond it. Our drainage article covers the rest of the yard.

How we set it out on site

  • Find the slab top at every door and the DPC line on each elevation, and mark the two lines on the brickwork with a string and a chalk line before any soil moves.
  • Decide the finish along each wall (paving, gravel strip, lawn) and mark the finished level for each from the lines above, not from the pad the builder left.
  • Set the fall from the wall with a string line and a level, then grade the pad to it before the surface goes down.
  • Lay the paving, then the gravel strip, then the beds, then the mulch, checking each against the chalk line.
  • Photograph the weepholes and the slab edge on the day we finish. It is the evidence that the levels were right at handover, and the owner’s guide for the next person who wants to mulch to the wall.

Questions people ask

  • The builder left the pad 300 mm below the slab. Do I have to fill it all?

    No, and you should not fill it to the slab. Fill to the line that suits the finish: paving can come up to 50 mm below the slab top if it falls away; soil and mulch stop 150 mm below it. The difference is made up with a step, a mowing strip or a low wall, not with more soil against the wall.

  • Can I mulch right up to the brickwork?

    Only if the top of the mulch is still 150 mm below the slab and below the weepholes. In practice that means a gravel or paved strip against the wall and the bed starting beyond it, which also keeps the termite inspection zone visible.

  • What about a rendered or cladded house?

    The lines are the same (slab top, DPC, weepholes, drainage fall). Render often hides the DPC, so we find it from the plans or the slab edge rather than from the wall.

  • My paving is flat against the house. Is that a problem?

    Yes. Paving needs 50 mm of fall over the first metre away from the wall (25 mm in low-rainfall areas), or water sits against the slab and finds the weepholes. Relaying the first course on a fall usually fixes it.

  • Do these rules apply to a suspended timber floor?

    Different numbers, same idea: the ground under the floor must sit above the finished ground outside and drain so nothing ponds under the house, and the subfloor vents must stay clear.
