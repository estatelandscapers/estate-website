When a concrete driveway or slab is built wrong

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

Concrete is the least forgiving thing we build. There is one chance to get it right, about ninety minutes long, and everything that decides whether it lasts thirty years or three has already happened before the truck arrives.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how concrete driveways and slabs commonly fail and how they are generally built, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with NSW Fair Trading and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. What failure looks like

  • Wide cracks, or cracks where one side sits higher than the other.
  • Water ponding on the slab, or running toward the garage or the house.
  • The surface flaking, dusting or wearing through to the stone.
  • Edges breaking away where car tyres leave the slab.
  • The slab pulling away from, or pushing against, the house.

Fine hairline cracks are normal. All concrete shrinks as it cures. Joints are there to make it crack in a straight line where you cannot see it.

The six ways concrete is built wrong

  1. Poured on unprepared ground. Topsoil, fill and soft clay settle under load. The ground has to be stripped to firm material and compacted, with a compacted base where it is needed. A slab is only as flat as what is under it.
  2. Too thin. Residential driveways are commonly 100 mm thick, and thicker where heavier vehicles use them. A slab that is 100 mm at the edge form and 60 mm in the middle is a common find when we break one out.
  3. Mesh on the ground. Steel mesh only works when it sits in the concrete, held up on chairs. Mesh laid on the dirt and "pulled up during the pour" ends up at the bottom, doing nothing.
  4. No joints, or joints in the wrong place. Control joints must be cut at regular spacing and at every change of shape. A flexible joint is needed where the slab meets the house, so the two can move separately.
  5. Wrong falls. The surface must fall away from the house to a drain or the street. Falls are set with the formwork. They cannot be corrected afterwards.
  6. Water added on site, and no curing. Adding water to the truck makes concrete easier to place and weaker for life. Concrete also needs to be kept from drying too fast in its first days, which matters most in a Sydney summer.

What it costs when it fails

Concrete cannot be topped up or patched to full strength. A failed driveway is cut up, broken out, loaded and tipped, and tipping concrete is charged by weight. The base is then done properly and the slab is poured again. Removal alone can approach the cost of the original pour.

How we build it

Where a slab is structural, or carries more than cars, such as a slab beside a retaining wall, over fill or for heavy vehicles, we build it to an engineer's design and have it signed off. For every slab the order of work is the same: strip and compact the ground, set the forms to the falls, place the mesh on chairs, order the concrete to the specified strength, cut the joints and cure it. On residential jobs over $20,000 we take out Home Building Compensation Fund cover and give you the certificate before work starts. We hold NSW contractor licence 487076C.

Questions people ask

  • How long before I can drive on it?

    Concrete gains strength over weeks. As a general guide, foot traffic after a day or two and cars after about a week, longer in cold weather. We tell you the dates for your pour.

  • My new driveway has a hairline crack. Is that a defect?

    Usually not. Fine shrinkage cracks are expected. A crack that is wide, growing, or stepped is different and worth having inspected.

  • Do I need council approval for the driveway?

    The section between your boundary and the road, the crossover, belongs to council and needs their approval. We cover that in a separate article.
