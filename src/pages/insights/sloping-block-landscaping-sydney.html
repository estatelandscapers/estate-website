Sloping block landscaping in Sydney: terraces, walls and access done properly

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 18 November 2026

You bought the block for the view. The house is finished, or nearly. Then the garden starts, and the slope that looked like character on the day of the auction becomes the hardest part of the whole project. A wall here, some steps there, a lawn squeezed onto the only flat patch. Two years and a lot of money later, the yard is still hard to use, the steps feel unsafe and nobody can get a machine to the bottom terrace to finish it.

What the problem looks like on a sloping block

On steep blocks in Castle Hill, Dural, Kenthurst, the Northern Beaches and the North Shore, we see the same pattern again and again:

  • Terraces that are too narrow to use. A level that fits a bench but not a table and chairs.
  • Walls built one at a time, at different heights, in different materials, with no consistent line.
  • Steps that are steep, uneven or slippery, with no handrail and a drop beside them.
  • Water running down the slope and across the lower terrace, the pool surround or the back of the house.
  • A finished upper level that blocks the only path a machine could have used to reach the bottom of the site.
  • A neighbour asking why their fence or garden has started to move.

Why sloping block landscaping goes wrong

Almost always, the slope was handled piece by piece instead of as one design. The builder set the house levels. The pool builder set the pool level. A landscaper was then asked to make the rest work. Together those decisions leave awkward steps between levels, walls in the wrong places and nowhere for the water to go.

The second cause is sequence. On a slope, the order of work matters as much as the work itself. Walls lower down usually have to be built before the levels above them are filled. Drainage has to go in behind each wall as it is built, not after. Heavy machines need a way in and a way out, and that path disappears as soon as the first terrace is paved. When nobody plans the sequence, work is redone, access is lost and the cost climbs.

The third cause is treating walls as garden features rather than structures. A terrace wall on a steep block holds back soil, water and whatever sits above it: paving, a pool fence, a spa, a driveway. That is engineering, and it needs to be designed as engineering.

What it costs when a slope is done in pieces

The expensive part is rarely the first wall. It is what has to come out to fix it. As an illustration only: a terrace wall that might cost around $40,000 to build properly at the start can cost two or three times that to replace once paving, a pool fence, lighting, irrigation and planting sit on top of it, and the only access is by hand or a small machine.

There are other costs that do not show on an invoice. Months without a usable garden. A yard that photographs badly when you come to sell. And on a boundary, a dispute with a neighbour. In NSW, the Conveyancing Act 1919 (section 177) sets out a duty of care not to remove the support that land provides to neighbouring land. Excavating a slope near a boundary without proper design and support is how owners end up in that conversation.

What to insist on before anyone digs

  1. A whole-site levels plan. One drawing showing every level, wall, step and fall from the house to the boundaries, tied to a survey. Not a sketch of the next stage.
  2. Engineering for the walls. Walls that retain real height, sit near a boundary or carry load above them should be designed by an engineer for your soil and what each wall supports. Ask who the engineer is and whether they will sign off the finished wall.
  3. A drainage plan that ends somewhere legal. Every wall needs drainage behind it, and all of it needs a lawful point of discharge. Water cannot simply be sent onto the neighbour below.
  4. An access and sequence plan. How machines get in, which levels are built first, where spoil and materials are stored, and how the last terrace gets finished.
  5. The approval pathway confirmed. Under the NSW exempt development code, earthworks and retaining walls are generally limited to 600 mm of cut or fill, kept at least 1 m from boundaries, and kept a minimum distance from any other wall. Most terraced prestige slopes go well beyond that and need a complying development certificate or a development application. Confirm which before the contract is signed.
  6. Safe movement designed in. The National Construction Code generally requires a barrier where a person could fall 1 m or more. On a terraced block that affects the look of every level, so it belongs in the design, not added at the end.

How a sloping block should be designed and built

Good sloping block landscaping starts with how you want to live on the site. Where the family eats outside. Where the pool sits relative to the house and the sun. Then the levels are set so the biggest, most-used spaces are generous and flat, and the slope is taken up by fewer, better walls and comfortable stairs.

Fewer, taller engineered walls often give a better result than many small ones. They create usable terraces instead of narrow strips. A planted batter or stepped walls with planting between can soften the look, checked by the engineer like any wall. Materials are chosen once for the whole site so the walls, steps and paving read as one design: natural stone, rendered masonry, or concrete sleeper systems faced to match the house.

Construction then follows the plan. Lower walls first, with drainage and free-draining backfill placed as each wall goes up. Services and lighting conduits laid before levels are closed. Access protected until the last machine has left. Paving, planting and finishes last, when the structure is complete and signed off.

Questions people ask

  • Is it better to terrace a sloping backyard or keep it natural?

    It depends on how you want to use the space. Terracing gives flat, usable areas for entertaining, lawn and a pool. Keeping more of the natural slope with planting can suit bushland and acreage blocks. Many of the best results combine both: generous terraces near the house and planted slope further out.

  • Do I need council approval to landscape a sloping block in NSW?

    Small works can be exempt, but the exempt code generally limits cut and fill to 600 mm and keeps walls at least 1 m from boundaries. Most terraced landscaping on a steep block goes beyond that and needs a complying development certificate or a development application. Check with your council or a certifier before work starts.

  • Do retaining walls on a sloping block need an engineer?

    Walls that retain real height, sit near a boundary or carry a load above them, such as paving, a pool or a driveway, should be designed by an engineer. On a steep site that is most of the walls.

  • How long does landscaping a steep block take?

    It depends on the approvals, the engineering, access and the weather. Approvals and design can take longer than the build itself. A realistic programme is set once the levels plan, engineering and access are known.

  • Can the landscaping be done at the same time as the house or pool?

    Often the best results come when the landscape levels are planned before the house and pool levels are fixed, even if the garden is built later. Bringing the landscape contractor in early protects access and avoids rework.
