The cheap quote costs three times: build it, remove it, build it again

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

A good share of our structural work is rebuilding something that is less than five years old. The owner paid once to have it built. They pay us to pull it out. Then they pay for the job they should have had the first time. The original quote was always the cheapest one on the table, and it usually came from someone without a licence for the work.

A summary, not advice

It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. What the licence actually is

In NSW, residential building work worth more than $5,000 in labour and materials must be done by a contractor licensed for that kind of work. Structural landscaping, which covers retaining walls, concrete, paving, decks, pergolas and drainage, falls under this. Above $5,000 there must also be a written contract, and the deposit cannot exceed 10 per cent. Above $20,000 the contractor must take out Home Building Compensation Fund (HBCF) cover for the job and give you the certificate before starting or taking money.

An unlicensed operator cannot get HBCF cover, usually has no public liability insurance that would respond, and will rarely put anything in writing. That is where the low price comes from.

Payment one: the build

The quote is 30 to 40 per cent under everyone else's. The saving is real, and it comes out of the parts you cannot see once the job is finished: the depth of the footings, the size of the steel, the drainage behind the wall, the base under the concrete, the engineer who was never engaged. The work looks fine on the day you pay.

Payment two: the removal

Structural work rarely fails in a way that can be patched. A wall that leans because the posts are too shallow cannot be straightened. A slab that cracked because there is no base under it cannot be topped. It has to come out, and demolition has its own costs: machine access through a now-finished yard, tipping fees for concrete and soil, and repairs to the fence, turf or paving that were built against it.

Payment three: the rebuild

The third payment is the price of doing it properly, the quote you turned down, usually a year or two later and at that year's prices. It often comes with extras the first job never had: an engineer's design, and sometimes an approval the original work needed and never got.

Why you usually cannot get the money back

  • No insurance to claim on. HBCF exists for when a contractor dies, disappears or becomes insolvent. Without a certificate there is no fund behind the job.
  • Nobody to pursue. NSW law gives homeowners statutory warranties on residential building work, but a warranty is only worth as much as the person who owes it. A cash operator with no licence, no company and a disconnected mobile is hard to find and harder to collect from.
  • Your own insurer may not help. Home insurance policies commonly exclude faulty workmanship, and may question damage caused by unapproved or non-compliant structures.
  • It follows the house. Council orders about unapproved work go to the current owner, and a building inspection at sale will find it.

Five checks that take ten minutes

  1. Ask for the licence number and look it up on the Service NSW licence check at verify.licence.nsw.gov.au. Check the name matches and the licence covers the type of work.
  2. Ask for the public liability certificate of currency.
  3. For jobs over $20,000, ask for the HBCF certificate before paying anything.
  4. Get a written contract with the scope, the price and the payment stages. Deposit no more than 10 per cent.
  5. For retaining walls and structures, ask who the engineer is.

Questions people ask

  • The job is under $5,000. Does any of this matter?

    The licence rule applies above $5,000, but a small job split into several invoices to stay under the line is still one job. And a 900 mm retaining wall can be under $5,000 and still hold up your neighbour's fence.

  • He says he works under a mate's licence.

    The contract has to be with the licence holder, in the name on the licence. If the name on the quote and the name on the licence do not match, you are not dealing with a licensed contractor.

  • Can you repair it instead of rebuilding?

    Sometimes. If the structure is sound and the fault is something like missing drainage, it can occasionally be fixed in place. We tell you honestly after an inspection, and an engineer makes the call on anything structural.
