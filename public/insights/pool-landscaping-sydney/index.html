Pool landscaping in Sydney: when the pool builder and the landscaper don't talk

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 25 November 2026

The pool shell is perfect. The tiles are perfect. Then the paving goes down and the coping sits 40 mm proud of the terrace, the stone falls towards the water, the glass fence spigots land on a drainage grate and the certifier will not sign off the barrier. Nobody did anything obviously wrong. The pool builder built to their plans and the landscaper built to theirs. The problem is the line where one contract ends and the other begins, and on a pool landscaping project in Sydney that line is where most of the money is lost.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how pool and landscape works commonly clash and how they are generally coordinated in NSW, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your pool design, your approval and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with Building a pool: your rights and obligations (NSW Government), the NSW Swimming Pool Register and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. For help with your project, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

What the problem looks like

  • The coping and the surrounding paving finish at different heights, so there is a step, a ramp or a mortar wedge where there should be one clean plane.
  • Paving falls towards the pool, so every storm washes leaves, soil and mulch into the water, or it falls towards the house with nowhere for the water to go.
  • The glass fence line was set before the paving layout, so spigots land on joints, grates or the edge of a step.
  • A garden bed, retaining wall or planter is built inside the 900 mm non-climbable zone and the barrier fails inspection.
  • The pool equipment, pipes and power were run where the landscape plan had a wall footing, a tree or a step.
  • The pool builder's machines and spoil wrecked the access path, and the landscaper's price assumed a clear site.

Why it happens

A premium pool area is usually two contracts on one piece of ground. The pool builder is responsible for the shell, the equipment and often the coping. The landscaper is responsible for everything that meets it: paving, walls, steps, drainage, planting and lighting. Each works from their own drawings. When nobody owns the finished levels, each trade assumes the other will adjust.

Three things make it worse on large projects. The pool is often designed and approved first, with the landscape added later. The coping is chosen by one party and the paving by another, in different stone and thickness. And the pool barrier is treated as a fence item at the end, when it is really a layout decision that controls where walls, beds and paving can go.

What it costs on a project of this size

On a pool area worth $150,000 or more, the fix is rarely cheap, because by the time the clash shows up the expensive materials are already down. As an illustrative example only: if the paving levels around a 10 by 4 metre pool are wrong by a few centimetres, putting it right can mean lifting the stone, losing a share of it to breakage, re-bedding, regrading the drainage and refitting the fence. On natural stone or large-format porcelain that can be a five-figure bill, often in the order of $30,000 to $60,000, before any delay to the handover or the summer you planned around. Fence relocations, rebuilt walls and failed barrier inspections add to it.

The harder cost is the argument. When two contractors each built to their own plans, working out who pays is slow, and the owner usually carries the cost while it is decided.

Pool landscaping in Sydney: what to settle before the pool is dug

Most of these problems are designed out, or designed in, before excavation. A professional checks these points while the pool is still on paper.

  1. One set of finished levels. A single plan that shows the coping height, the paving level, the falls, the house floor level and every step, agreed by both contractors before either starts.
  2. A written scope line. Each contract should say exactly where it stops: who supplies and lays the coping, who does the waterproofing and drainage under the surround, who sets out the fence and who restores access after the pool works.
  3. The barrier on the plan. The fence line, gates and the 900 mm non-climbable zone drawn on the landscape plan, so walls, beds and planting are placed around it. Our pool fence rules article sets out the numbers.
  4. Services and equipment. Where the pump, filter, heater, pipes and power go, and that they clear wall footings, tree roots and future lighting.
  5. Ground conditions. The NSW Government guidance on building a pool recommends a geotechnical survey, because rock or unstable soil can change the cost. The same report informs any retaining walls and footings around the pool.
  6. Licences, contracts and cover for each party. In NSW, work over $5,000 needs a licensed contractor and a written contract, the deposit is capped at 10%, and a pool project over $20,000 needs a Home Building Compensation Fund certificate before the builder asks for money. Check that each contractor's licence covers the work in their contract.

One caution for owners who like to run their own projects: the NSW Government notes that if you coordinate two or more contractors to build the pool itself and the work is over $10,000, you will need an owner-builder permit, and owner-builders cannot get HBCF cover. Engaging a pool builder for the pool and a separate licensed landscaper for the garden is common. Splitting the pool itself between trades is a different thing.

How it should be done

The pool and the landscape should be designed together, even if they are built by different companies. The sequence that works on a large project is:

  1. Landscape and pool concept drawn on one survey, with levels and the barrier line.
  2. Pool approved (a pool needs a complying development certificate or a development application), with the landscape works that affect the approval shown.
  3. Engineering for any retaining walls, raised terraces or structural slabs near the pool, coordinated with the pool engineer so footings and the shell do not load each other.
  4. Pool built, with landscape drainage and conduits laid before the surrounds close up.
  5. Coping and surrounds laid to the agreed levels, then the barrier installed and inspected, then planting, lighting and finishing.

Drainage deserves its own line. Surface water from the surround should fall away from the pool to grated drains, connected to a lawful stormwater point. Pool water from the filter is a separate system and is connected by a licensed plumber as the approval requires. Our yard drainage article explains how surface drainage fails.

Questions people ask

  • Should the pool builder or the landscaper do the paving around the pool?

    Either can work. What matters is that one party owns the finished levels and the contracts say who lays the coping and who lays the surround. Problems start when both assume the other is matching them.

  • When should I bring the landscaper into a pool project?

    Before the pool design is final. Levels, the barrier line, walls and services are fixed when the pool is dug, and changing them afterwards is where the cost is.

  • Who is responsible if water runs into the pool or pools against the coping?

    It depends on what each contract and the agreed level plan said. If there was no shared level plan, it is often unclear, which is why it should be settled in writing before work starts.

  • Does landscaping around a new pool need HBCF cover?

    In NSW, residential building work over $20,000 generally needs Home Building Compensation Fund cover, and a pool project over $20,000 needs a certificate from the pool builder before any money is requested. Check each contract separately with the contractor and the NSW Government guidance.
