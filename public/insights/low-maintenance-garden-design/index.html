A low-maintenance garden is designed, not bought

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

Nobody asks us for a high-maintenance garden. Yet most of the gardens we are called back to rescue were sold as low maintenance. The difference is rarely the plants. It is whether the garden was designed so that the work never needs doing, or just planted and left for the owner to keep up with.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how a garden can be designed to need less upkeep in Sydney conditions, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with NSW Fair Trading and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. For help with your block, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

Low maintenance means fewer jobs, not no jobs

Every garden takes some work. The aim is to remove the jobs that repeat every week and keep only the ones that happen a few times a year. Mowing, edging, weeding, hand watering and hedge trimming are the weekly jobs. A good design takes most of them away before the first plant goes in.

The six decisions that set the workload

  1. Fix the ground first. On a new build the builder usually leaves compacted clay. Plants in clay struggle, and struggling plants are the high-maintenance ones. Ripping the subsoil, adding a proper depth of garden mix and getting the levels to drain is the least visible and most important part of the job.
  2. Put the right plant in the right spot. A plant that suits the sun, shade, wind and soil it is given needs almost nothing. A plant that doesn't will need water, feeding, spraying and eventually replacing. We choose from what already thrives in Sydney: lomandra, dianella, westringia, native grasses, and tough exotics like liriope and star jasmine.
  3. Plant for the mature size. Most pruning exists because something was planted where it could never fit. If a shrub grows to two metres, give it two metres. A plant that fits its space is pruned once a year, if at all.
  4. Plant in masses. Five species in large groups is easier to look after than thirty species planted one each. Dense planting shades the soil, which is what stops weeds. Bare mulch between scattered plants is an invitation.
  5. Build hard edges. Steel, concrete or stone edging between lawn and garden means the mower does the edging. Without it, grass runs into the beds and someone is on their knees every month.
  6. Automate the water. Drip irrigation under the mulch on a timer waters the roots, not the leaves or the path. It uses less water than a hose and removes the one job people forget in January.

Mulch, and what it sits on

A 75 mm layer of coarse mulch holds moisture and blocks light from weed seeds. It breaks down and needs a top-up every year or two, which is the main recurring cost of a garden bed. We do not lay weed mat under organic mulch in planted beds. It stops the mulch feeding the soil, weeds root into the layer on top of it within a couple of seasons, and it is miserable to remove. Where pebbles are used instead of mulch, a geotextile underneath is the right call.

Be honest about the lawn

Lawn is the highest-maintenance surface in most yards. Keep it where it is used, for children, dogs and somewhere to sit, and give the awkward strips down the side of the house to pebbles, pavers or ground cover. Where there is lawn, choose a variety for the amount of sun it actually gets and give it a simple shape a mower can follow.

The first two years decide the next twenty

No garden is low maintenance on day one. New plants need regular water through their first summer and the weeds need pulling while the planting closes over. After roughly two growing seasons the garden covers its own soil, and from then on the work drops to a seasonal tidy, a mulch top-up and an irrigation check.

Questions people ask

  • Are native plants always low maintenance?

    No. Natives suited to your soil and aspect are very low maintenance. A native from a different climate planted in heavy clay is not. Suitability matters more than origin.

  • Is artificial turf the lowest-maintenance option?

    It removes mowing and watering, but it still needs brushing and cleaning, it gets hot in full sun, and it has a limited life. It suits small shaded areas where real grass won't grow.

  • Are pebbles easier than mulch?

    Pebbles don't break down, so there is no top-up. They do collect leaves and need blowing clean, and they hold heat. We use them close to the house and under eaves, and mulch in planted beds.
