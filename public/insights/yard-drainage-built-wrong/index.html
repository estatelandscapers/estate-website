When yard drainage is built wrong

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

Bad drainage is the defect nobody sees at handover. The yard looks finished and the pits have shiny grates. Then the first east coast low arrives, and the water shows you exactly what was built: where the ground falls, where the pipes go, and whether they go anywhere at all.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how yard drainage commonly fails and how it is generally built in NSW, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with NSW Fair Trading and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. For help with your block, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

What failure looks like

  • Water sitting against the house, or on the lawn, a day after rain.
  • Damp, mould or a musty smell inside along an outside wall.
  • Pits that fill and overflow, or never see any water at all.
  • Water running into the neighbour's yard, or theirs into yours.
  • Soil washing out from under paving, or a retaining wall weeping mud.

The five ways drainage is built wrong

  1. The ground falls the wrong way. Pipes and pits are the backup. The first line of drainage is the shape of the ground, which must fall away from the house. Garden beds and paving built up against the wall, covering the weepholes, send water into the building.
  2. Pits in the wrong place. A pit only works at the low point. A pit placed where it was convenient to dig sits dry while the water ponds two metres away.
  3. Pipes that go nowhere. Every drain needs a legal point of discharge, usually the street kerb, an easement pipe or the property's stormwater system. We regularly find pipes that end under the lawn, in a hole filled with rubble, or connected to the sewer, which is not permitted.
  4. Pipes too small or too flat. A pipe needs enough fall along its length to carry water and clear itself of silt. Flat or back-falling pipes fill with mud within a few seasons.
  5. Ag line without fabric or gravel. Slotted subsoil pipe laid straight in clay blocks quickly. It needs to be wrapped, bedded in clean gravel, and given somewhere to drain to.

What it costs when it fails

Drainage is the first thing in the ground and the last thing anyone wants to dig up. Fixing it means lifting the turf, paving or garden that was laid over it. The larger cost is what the water does in the meantime: damp in the house, movement in slabs and footings on reactive clay, a failed retaining wall, or a dispute with the neighbour downhill. In NSW you are generally expected not to redirect or concentrate water onto next door.

How we build it

We design the levels before anything else, so the surface does most of the work and the pipes handle the rest. Pits go at the low points, pipes are laid to fall, and every line runs to a lawful discharge point. Stormwater connections are licensed plumbing and drainage work and are done by a licensed drainer. Where drainage is part of a structure, such as behind a retaining wall or under a slab, it is built to the engineer's design and covered by their sign-off. On residential jobs over $20,000 we take out Home Building Compensation Fund cover and give you the certificate before work starts. We hold NSW contractor licence 487076C.

Questions people ask

  • Will an ag line fix a boggy lawn?

    Sometimes. If the problem is surface water with nowhere to go, regrading and a pit will do more. If it is water held in clay subsoil, subsoil drainage and better soil help. We work out which it is first.

  • My neighbour's water runs into my yard. What can I do?

    Natural flow downhill is generally something the lower owner has to manage. Water that has been redirected or concentrated by building work is different. Start with a conversation and photos taken during rain.

  • There is a stormwater pit or easement in my backyard. Can I build over it?

    Usually not without approval. We have a separate article on easements and pits.
