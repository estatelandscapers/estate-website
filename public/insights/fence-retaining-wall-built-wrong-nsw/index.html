The fence or retaining wall was built wrong. Whose problem is it?

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 7 November 2026

Half the walls and fences we are asked to fix were not built by the person asking. A previous owner, the neighbour, a builder who has moved on. The law does not care who you bought from; it asks three things: where is the structure, does it do its job, and was the right process followed. Each has a different answer about who pays.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how NSW law generally treats a fence or retaining wall that was built in the wrong place, built badly or built without the right process, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, the law and the cases change, and your title, your contract of sale and the history of the work can all change the answer. Read the Legal Aid NSW guidance, the Dividing Fences Act 1991, the Encroachment of Buildings Act 1922 and the Home Building Act 1989 on the NSW legislation website, and get legal advice before you act on any of it. We are landscapers, not lawyers. For help working out what the wall or fence needs and what it costs to fix, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

Three ways a wall or fence is "wrong"

  • Wrong place. The fence is not on the boundary, or the retaining wall crosses it. A survey decides this, not the fence.
  • Wrong build. The wall leans, drains badly, was never engineered, or was built without the approval it needed. Fences fall over; walls fail slowly and expensively.
  • Wrong process. Someone built on the line without a notice, an agreement or an order, then asked the neighbour for money, or built a wall that took the neighbour’s support away.

Who built it matters less than you would think. When you buy land you buy the structures on it, and their problems. What you get with the land is a set of rights against the people who built it, if they are still in time.

Wrong place

The boundary is where the registered plan and a surveyor put it. A fence built off the line for thirty years does not move it. Under the Dividing Fences Act either owner can require a sufficient fence on the boundary: serve a fencing notice, and if there is no agreement after a month, apply to the Local Court or NCAT for a fencing order. The new fence is shared; the order decides who carries the cost of removing the old one.

A retaining wall over the line is an encroachment, and the Encroachment of Buildings Act 1922 is the law for it. Either owner can apply to the Land and Environment Court, which can order compensation to the owner whose land is taken, transfer the strip or grant an easement to the encroaching owner, or order the wall removed. Compensation starts at the value of the land taken and can be up to three times that where the encroaching owner cannot show the encroachment was neither intentional nor negligent. Courts are slow to order a working wall torn out for a small strip, which is what happened in a 2022 Supreme Court case where the wall stayed, works were ordered to restore support and damages followed. Also check Part 14A of the Real Property Act: a boundary determination can settle where the line is before anyone argues about what is over it.

Wrong build

A retaining wall belongs to the land it stands on and the owner whose earthworks it holds back, whoever built it. If it was built badly by a previous owner, you own the problem. Your ways of recovering the cost are:

  • The builder. The Home Building Act statutory warranties (work done with due care and skill, suitable materials, compliance with the law) run for six years for major defects and two years for other defects from completion, and they pass to later owners as successors in title. A failing retaining wall that is part of the house’s support is the kind of thing argued as a major defect. Get the builder’s name and the completion date from the previous owner’s documents or council.
  • The seller. Only where the contract of sale gave a warranty, or the seller misrepresented the work. The standard NSW contract gives little; a buyer’s own checks before exchange are the real protection.
  • Nobody. A wall built by an owner-builder twenty years ago, with the approvals in order, is simply yours to maintain.

If a wall on the neighbour’s land is failing and your land is slumping or flooding, the Conveyancing Act duty of support and the law of nuisance put the repair on the neighbour, and the damage too. Photograph, measure, get an engineer, write to them, and get legal advice before the first excavator arrives. Most retaining wall disputes that do not settle go to the Supreme Court, so settling is the aim.

Wrong process

A neighbour who builds a dividing fence without serving a notice or reaching an agreement cannot make you pay for it, unless it was urgent work. You can still require the fence to be on the boundary and sufficient. The same notice rules, in reverse, are what keep your own claim alive: notice first, build second. Our who-pays article sets out the notice step by step.

A wall built without the approval it needed is a council matter as well as a neighbour one. Walls above 600 mm, or closer than the exempt rules allow, need a complying development certificate or a DA. Council can issue orders under the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act to repair, alter or demolish unauthorised work, and the order goes to the owner at the time, not the person who built it. A building information certificate, usually with a structural engineer’s certification, asks council to accept the work as it stands; once issued, council agrees not to order its demolition or alteration for seven years. Buyers ask for one before settlement for exactly this reason.

The order to do things in

  • Survey. Peg the boundary before anyone argues about what is on which side of it.
  • Engineer. An independent structural report on what the wall does, what it supports, whether it supports the fence, and what it costs to fix. Everything after this depends on it.
  • Records. Council’s DA and CDC history for the lot, the planning certificate, the previous owner’s building documents, the builder’s name and the completion date. They tell you whether a warranty claim is still open and whether council is a factor.
  • Write to the other owner with the survey and the report, and what you propose.
  • Mediation through a Community Justice Centre, free and quick.
  • Notice and orders. A fencing notice for anything the Dividing Fences Act covers; the Land and Environment Court for an encroachment; the Supreme Court for a wall dispute the Act does not cover, with legal advice first.
  • Fix it once. Engineered, approved where it needs to be, drained, and documented, so the next owner inherits a wall and not a dispute.

Questions people ask

  • The previous owner built the retaining wall without council approval. Can council make me remove it?

    Yes. Orders go to the current owner. Before it gets to that, ask council about a building information certificate with an engineer’s certification; if the wall is sound it is usually accepted. If it is not sound, you are rebuilding it anyway.

  • The neighbour’s fence is 300 mm onto my land. Can I move it?

    Not on your own. Serve a fencing notice proposing a sufficient fence on the surveyed boundary; if there is no agreement in a month, NCAT or the Local Court can order it. Removing the old fence yourself invites a trespass argument.

  • Can I claim the cost of fixing a previous owner’s wall from them?

    Usually not. Your claim is against the builder under the Home Building Act if the wall is within six years of completion for a major defect, or against the seller only if the contract gave a warranty or they misrepresented it.

  • The neighbour built the fence and now wants half from me. I was never asked.

    Without a notice, an agreement or an order you are not liable to contribute, unless the work was genuinely urgent. You can still agree to pay if the fence is one you would have wanted.

  • My retaining wall is holding up the neighbour’s fill, built before I bought. Whose wall is it?

    If it is on your land it is yours to maintain, but the duty of support means the neighbour cannot add load or alter their levels in a way that overloads it. If their fill was placed after the wall was built, get an engineer and legal advice: the answer can shift.
