When a retaining wall is built wrong

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

A retaining wall holds back tonnes of soil, and after rain, the water in it. It does this every day for decades. When one is built wrong it rarely collapses overnight. It leans a little more each wet season until the fence on top is crooked and the neighbour is at the door.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how retaining walls commonly fail and how they are generally built in NSW, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with NSW Fair Trading and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. For help with your block, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

What failure looks like

  • The top of the wall leans out, and the lean is worse than last year.
  • Sleepers bow between the posts, or crack through the middle.
  • Soil or muddy water comes through the joints after rain.
  • The ground behind the wall sinks, or a gap opens between the wall and the soil.
  • The fence, paving or pool coping above the wall moves with it.

The five ways walls are built wrong

  1. No drainage. This is the cause of most failures. Without an ag line at the base and free-draining gravel behind the wall, water builds up in the soil and its weight is added to the load. A wall designed for damp soil is suddenly holding back a swimming pool.
  2. Posts too shallow. The depth of the post in the ground is what resists the wall tipping over. A common shortcut is to stop digging when the auger hits rock or gets difficult. The post depth and footing size are an engineering decision, set by the height and the soil.
  3. Posts too light or too far apart. Steel posts are sized for the height of the wall and what it carries. Undersized steel bends slowly and permanently.
  4. Ignoring what sits above. A driveway, a pool, a shed, a sloping yard or a fence on top all add load, called surcharge. A wall designed for level garden behind it will not hold a parked car.
  5. No engineering, no approval. In NSW a retaining wall above a low height, or close to a boundary, generally needs an engineer's design and an approval. Walls built without either are the ones that fail, and the ones councils order removed.

What it costs when it fails

The wall is the smaller part of the bill. The fence built on it, the turf and paving above it, the neighbour's yard below it and the machine access to get it out all have to be paid for. A wall on or near a boundary also brings the neighbour into the conversation, which is covered in our article on who pays.

How we build it

Our structural retaining walls are built to an engineer's design: post size and spacing, footing depth and diameter, drainage and backfill are specified for your site and what the wall has to carry, not copied from the last job. The engineer signs off on the completed wall, and you keep that paperwork for council, for your insurer and for the day you sell. On residential jobs over $20,000 we take out Home Building Compensation Fund cover and give you the certificate before work starts. We hold NSW contractor licence 487076C.

Questions people ask

  • My wall leans a little. Is it failing?

    Some walls are built with a slight lean back into the soil on purpose. A lean outward, or one that is getting worse, needs looking at. Put a level or a string line on it, photograph it, and check again after the next heavy rain.

  • Can a leaning wall be fixed without rebuilding?

    Rarely. If the posts are too shallow or too light, nothing added later changes that. Sometimes drainage can be retrofitted to stop a sound wall getting worse. An engineer decides.

  • Timber or concrete sleepers?

    Treated timber has a limited life in the ground. Concrete sleepers in galvanised steel posts cost more and last far longer. For anything structural or on a boundary we recommend concrete.
