Insights · New builds: levels and drainage

How high can the garden and paving sit against the house?

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 7 November 2026

The most common fault we fix on a landscaped new build is not a wall or a drain. It is soil and mulch piled against the house, over the weepholes and the damp-proof course, because nobody set a level before the yard went in. That is why we only ever put concrete or pebbles against a building, never a garden bed. The house tells you where the lines are. This is how to read them, and the two sections we set out from.

A summary, not advice

This article is generic information about finished levels around a house in NSW, drawn from the National Construction Code Housing Provisions and the Australian Standards it calls up, as we understand them at the date of publishing. It may or may not apply to your property: the code changes, soil class, rainfall area, termite management, flood level and the builder’s own details can all move the numbers, and the figures on your building plans and in your builder’s handover documents take precedence over anything here. Read the current NCC Housing Provisions on drainage (Part 3.3) and weatherproofing masonry (Part 5.7), and the NSW Building Commission note on damp-proof courses. For expert advice on your block, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

Four lines on the wall

Stand at the outside of the house and there are four levels you are working to, from the top down:

  • The slab top (the inside floor level). Under the NCC Housing Provisions the slab must sit at least 150 mm above finished ground, which can drop to 100 mm in sandy, well-drained or low-rainfall ground, and to 50 mm above paving or concrete that falls away from the house.
  • The damp-proof course (DPC), the membrane in the brickwork a few courses up from the slab, with the flashing. It has to stay above the outside level: generally 150 mm above finished ground, reducing to 75 mm above paved, concreted or landscaped areas that slope away from the wall, and lower only where the wall is sheltered by a carport or verandah. Bury the DPC and the brick below it wicks water into the wall.
  • The weepholes: the open perpend joints in the course above the flashing, at least 50 mm high and no more than 1.2 m apart. They drain the cavity. They must stay open and above the outside level, and not be filled by mulch, soil, render or a paver laid against them.
  • The slab edge and the rebate: the bricks sit in a rebate below the floor level, and where the slab edge is the termite barrier (most NSW new builds) AS 3660.1 wants 75 mm of that edge, below the bottom of the bricks, kept visible so an inspection can see any termite tracks. Garden soil or paving over the edge defeats the barrier. On a rebated slab this is usually the line that sets the outside level, because 75 mm below the brick base is already more than 50 mm below the floor.

Paving against the house

Paving and concrete can come closest, because they shed water. Lay the first course at least 75 mm below the base of the bricks (so the slab edge stays visible), which on a rebated slab puts it well over the 50 mm the code asks for below the floor level, with a fall of 50 mm over the first metre away from the wall (25 mm is allowed for impermeable surfaces in low-rainfall areas, which most of Sydney is not). That keeps the DPC comfortably more than 75 mm clear and keeps the weepholes open. Where the paving has to meet a door threshold, the threshold is the slab top, so the paving still steps down from it; a grated channel drain at the door is how a level threshold is done, not raising the paving.

Pebbles against the house

Where the finish is not concrete or paving, we run a pebble or gravel strip along the wall: 300 to 400 mm wide, on a compacted base over weed mat, with an ag drain under it where the yard falls towards the house. Pebbles drain through, so the code treats them like a landscaped area rather than paving: the floor level stays 150 mm above the pebble surface (100 mm in sandy, well-drained ground), the DPC 75 mm above it where it falls away, and the brick base 75 mm above it for the termite inspection. Lawn and garden beds start past the strip. We do not put soil or mulch against a wall: it holds water, it creeps up over the weepholes within a season, and it hides the slab edge.

The fall, and where the water goes

Levels only work with a fall. Every finished surface in the first metre from the house falls away from it, and from there the yard keeps falling to a pit, a swale or the street, never back to the slab. On a block that slopes towards the house the first metre still falls away; a drain or a retaining wall picks the water up beyond it. Our drainage article covers the rest of the yard.

The two sections we set out from

On most NSW brick veneer homes the outer brick leaf sits in a rebate at the slab edge, so the base of the bricks is lower than the floor inside. The flashing comes from the frame, crosses the cavity and comes out through the first brick course above the rebate; the weepholes are in that course. Below the bricks, the slab edge is what the termite inspector needs to see. The outside level is set from those three things, not from the floor you can see through the door.

How we set it out on site

  • Find the slab top at every door and the DPC line on each elevation, and mark the two lines on the brickwork with a string and a chalk line before any soil moves.
  • Decide the finish along each wall (concrete, paving or a pebble strip) and mark the finished level for each from the lines above, not from the pad the builder left.
  • Set the fall from the wall with a string line and a level, then grade the pad to it before the surface goes down.
  • Lay the concrete or paving, then the pebble strip, then the beds beyond it, checking each against the chalk line.
  • Photograph the weepholes and the slab edge on the day we finish. It is the evidence that the levels were right at handover, and the owner’s guide for the next person who wants to garden up to the wall.

Questions people ask

  • The builder left the pad 300 mm below the slab. Do I have to fill it all?

    No, and you should not fill it to the slab. Fill to the line that suits the finish against the wall: concrete or paving to 75 mm below the brick base, pebbles to 150 mm below the floor level. The difference is made up with a step, a mowing strip or a low wall, not with more soil against the wall.

  • Can I run a garden bed right up to the brickwork?

    We do not. Soil and mulch hold water against the wall and creep up over the weepholes. A pebble or concrete strip against the wall with the bed starting beyond it keeps the DPC, the weepholes and the termite inspection zone clear, and it is what we build on every job.

  • What about a rendered or cladded house?

    The lines are the same (slab top, DPC, weepholes, drainage fall). Render often hides the DPC, so we find it from the plans or the slab edge rather than from the wall.

  • My paving is flat against the house. Is that a problem?

    Yes. Paving needs 50 mm of fall over the first metre away from the wall (25 mm in low-rainfall areas), or water sits against the slab and finds the weepholes. Relaying the first course on a fall usually fixes it.

  • Do these rules apply to a suspended timber floor?

    Different numbers, same idea: the ground under the floor must sit above the finished ground outside and drain so nothing ponds under the house, and the subfloor vents must stay clear.
