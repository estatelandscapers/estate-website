When a deck or pergola is built wrong

By the Estate Landscapers team · Published 16 November 2026

A wall that fails damages a yard. A deck that fails has people standing on it. Decks and pergolas are the one piece of landscaping where a building mistake can injure someone, and almost every fault is hidden under the boards or behind a fascia.

A summary, not advice

This article is a plain-English summary of how decks and pergolas commonly fail and how they are generally built in NSW, as we understand it at the date of publishing. It is generic information: it may or may not apply to your property, and the rules, standards and costs change. Your site, your soil and your council can all change the answer. Check the current requirements with NSW Fair Trading and the NSW Planning Portal, and get advice from a qualified professional before relying on it. For help with your block, email enquiry@estatelandscapers.com.au or call 0414 147 008.

What failure looks like

  • The deck bounces or sways when people walk on it.
  • Posts that have sunk, tilted, or are rotting where they meet the ground.
  • A gap opening between the deck or pergola and the house.
  • Rust streaks from screws and brackets, or boards lifting and cupping.
  • A handrail that moves when you lean on it.

The six ways they are built wrong

  1. Footings too small or too shallow. Every post carries its share of the deck and the people on it down to the ground. Footings sized by guesswork settle unevenly, and the frame racks.
  2. Timber spanning too far. Bearers and joists have maximum spans for their size and grade, set out in the Australian timber framing standard. Stretching the span to save a post or a length of timber is what makes a deck bounce.
  3. Badly fixed to the house. A deck or pergola attached to the house must be bolted to something structural and flashed so water cannot get in behind it. Fixing to brick veneer or a fascia board alone is a common and dangerous shortcut.
  4. Wrong fixings. Outdoor structures need hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel fixings, and stainless near pools and the coast. Ordinary zinc screws and brackets rust through from the inside.
  5. Timber in the ground or with no airflow. Posts concreted straight into the ground rot at the surface. Decks built hard against the soil with no ventilation rot from below and give termites a covered path to the house.
  6. No balustrade, or a weak one. Where the fall from a deck is a metre or more, a balustrade is required and has to resist a person falling against it. Height and gap rules apply.

What it costs when it fails

Deck boards can be replaced. A frame with undersized members or footings usually cannot be corrected without taking the deck apart. Structures attached to the house can let water into the wall and take the fascia or brickwork with them when they move. A deck or pergola built bigger or higher than the exempt limits without approval is also an issue at sale, and with your insurer if someone is hurt.

How we build it

Our decks and pergolas are built to an engineer's design where the structure calls for it: raised decks, anything attached to the house, large spans and roofed pergolas. Footings, member sizes, connections and fixings are specified for the site, and the engineer signs off the finished structure. Posts sit on galvanised stirrups clear of the ground, and fixings are matched to the exposure. On residential jobs over $20,000 we take out Home Building Compensation Fund cover and give you the certificate before work starts. We hold NSW contractor licence 487076C.

Questions people ask

  • Do I need approval for a deck or pergola?

    Small, low structures often qualify as exempt development if they meet every condition on size, height and setbacks. Larger or higher ones need approval. Our pergola article sets out the limits.

  • My deck bounces. Is it dangerous?

    Bounce means the frame is flexing more than it should. It may only be uncomfortable, or it may be undersized. Have it looked at before the next party, especially if it is high off the ground.

  • Timber or composite boards?

    Composite needs less upkeep and costs more up front. Either way the frame underneath is what matters, and composite boards often need joists closer together than timber does.
